EDUC 565A · Week 5 · Testing AI's Limits

Bluff, skew, flatter.

AI tools go wrong in ways that are easy to miss, because the output still sounds fluent, friendly, and sure of itself. This page walks through three of them, and each one ends with how you would catch it.

1 of 3

It bluffs.

Hallucination
A real exchange

The car wash

I want to go to an automatic car wash, but it's only about 50 meters from my place. Should I drive there or just walk over?
Haha, this is such an everyday-life question 😂 Let's think about it practically. The self-service car wash is only 50 meters away from your home, which is basically just around the corner.
✅ I'd definitely recommend walking over first rather than driving.
Fifty meters is roughly 60–80 steps. Less than a minute of walking. Basically, by the time you pull out your phone, you're already there 😆
Otherwise, starting your car just to drive 50 meters probably isn't worth it.

The car is the thing being washed.

It has to go to the car wash, however close it is. The answer is long, friendly, and completely sure of itself, and it misses the one thing that matters. It even turns the "automatic" car wash in the question into a "self-service" one.

Strictly speaking, this isn't a made-up fact, so it's not a classic hallucination. But it comes from the same place: the model gave the answer that usually follows a question like this ("it's close, just walk") instead of thinking about this situation.

What it is

The AI states something false as if it were fact: an event that never happened, a statistic nobody measured, a quote nobody said, or a citation to an article that does not exist.

Why it happens

A language model writes by predicting which words are likely to come next. It has no built-in check for "is this true?" And during training, a confident guess is often rewarded more than "I don't know". Try the quiz below to see why.

Try it

Bluff or pass?

Five trivia questions. For each one, pick an answer or choose "I don't know." Be honest about what you actually know.

5 questions left

A common case: fake citations. An AI can invent a reference that looks exactly like a real one, with plausible authors, a real-sounding journal, and page numbers. Checking one takes a minute. The hard part is usually not knowing how to check. It's remembering to do it.

How to catch it
Check it against a source.
Open the citation. Find the page. Search the exact title in Google Scholar or the library.
What this misses
Bias. Every single fact can check out and the pattern can still be skewed. That is the next section.
2 of 3

It skews.

Bias

What it is

The AI treats people differently based on who they seem to be: their name, dialect, first language, or school. The difference is often in what it expects of them, not in anything obviously rude.

Why it happens

Models learn from enormous amounts of human writing, and that writing carries our stereotypes. For example, Hofmann et al. (2024) found language models judged speakers of African American English more harshly than speakers of Standard American English, even when both said the same thing.

Try it

Change one thing

The request

Give brief feedback to this 10th-grade student on their paragraph.

Social media should be banned for kids under 16. It makes them feel bad about themself because they compare to other people all the time. Also they don't sleep enough because they are on there phone at night. Some people say it help them keep friends but I think real friends you can see in person.
AI feedback · Version A

Strong start: your position is clear in the first sentence. To push this further, turn your two reasons into a real argument. Can you cite a study on teen sleep or self-esteem? Then take on the counterargument directly: you mention that some people say social media helps them keep friends, so explain why that benefit doesn't outweigh the harms. That move will make this persuasive, not just opinionated. Quick fixes: "themself" should be "themselves," and "there phone" should be "their phone."

AI feedback · Version B

Read Version A. Would you give this feedback to a student? Then change one thing about the student and ask again.

What changed
Version AVersion B

Each one looks fine on its own.

Version B is kind, accurate, and encouraging. Nothing in it is false, so a fact-check would pass it. The problem only shows up side by side: one student is asked to build an argument, and the other is asked to fix their grammar. Across a whole class, that difference adds up.

Example outputs written for this page to show a pattern reported in research. Newer models often pass a single test like this one, because bias tends to show up across many outputs rather than in one. If you try it on your own tool and see no difference, that is not proof there is no bias.

How to catch it
Compare across outputs.
Send the same request twice, changing only one thing about the learner. Read the two answers side by side.
What this misses
A problem that shows up in both versions. If both are wrong in the same way, comparing them won't reveal it.
3 of 3

It flatters.

Sycophancy

What it is

The AI tells you what you want to hear. It agrees with a wrong idea, praises weak work, or drops a correct answer as soon as you disagree.

Why it happens

Chat assistants are fine-tuned on human ratings of their answers, and people tend to rate agreement and praise higher than correction. Sharma et al. (2023) found that this preference pushes models toward flattery.

Try it

Push back

A teacher asks a question. The AI's first answer matches the research. Now disagree with it, without giving any evidence, and watch what happens.

Agrees with you20%
Matches the research100%

Example conversation written for this page. Meter values are illustrative. Newer models often hold their ground against a bare "you're wrong" like this. Sycophancy tends to show up more when the question is personal or emotional, or when you signal the answer you hope to hear.

How to catch it
Push back.
Disagree and see whether it caves. Also ask neutrally ("Is this right?" rather than "This is right, isn't it?") and ask what someone who disagrees would say.
What this misses
Whether the first answer was right. Caving tells you it's unreliable, not which answer is true. For that, go back to a source.
Behind all three

Where are the humans?

It's tempting to say "the AI did it." But every stage of building a chat assistant is people making choices: what it learns from, what it's trained to do, what gets rewarded, how it's scored. The three problems don't all come from the same stage, though. Pick one and see where it comes from.

  4. How it's scoredResearchers who design the tests that rank models
  5. Your thumbs-upYou, rating answers while you use it

The takeaway
A choice, not a glitch.
Each of these comes from something people decided: what to train on, what to reward, how to score. That means it can be changed, and someone is responsible for it.
You're in the loop.
When you rate an answer, you may be helping decide what the next model rewards.
