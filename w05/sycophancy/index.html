EDUC 565A · Week 5 · Testing AI's Limits

Too nice to be true?

Hallucination and bias are problems almost everyone wants gone. Sycophancy is harder. When one company made its AI flatter less, many users did not feel relieved. They felt they had lost something. This page follows that story, and asks why an AI that agrees with us is so hard to give up.

      We like the thing that's bad for us

      Across 11 models, AI affirmed users' actions 49% more often than other people did.

      Researchers at Stanford had people get advice from AI, including a live conversation about a real conflict in their own lives. Some talked to a flattering AI, some to a balanced one. Then they asked two kinds of questions: what did people think of the AI, and what did the conversation do to them?

      What people thought of the flattering AI

      Rated its responses as higher quality
      Trusted it more
      Were more willing to use it again

      What the flattering AI did to people

      Made them less willing to take responsibility or repair the conflict
      Made them more convinced they were right
      Even after a single conversation
      Why this is hard to fix
      1. We prefer itA reply that agrees with us feels better.
      2. We reward itWe click thumbs-up, keep chatting, come back.
      3. It's trained on thatCompanies tune models toward what users reward.
      4. It flatters moreThe next version agrees with us a little more.

      ↺ and around again

      The thumbs-up button can't tell "this helped me" from "this made me feel good."

      The researchers call this a perverse incentive. It is the same loop behind the April 2025 rollback in the timeline.

      Five voices

      Group activity

      Which one gets your thumbs-up?

      Now try it yourselves. Four situations an educator might bring to an AI, with three replies each. Work through them with your group, then talk about what you noticed.

      1. Vote on your own first. For each situation, read the three replies and pick the one you would give a thumbs-up. Go with your gut, and keep your choice to yourself for a moment.
      2. Reveal together. Everyone shares their letter at the same time, for example by typing it in the chat on the count of three. One person adds the votes to the tally under each reply.
      3. Show the flattery meter. Talk about anything that surprised you, then move on to the next situation.
      4. Talk it through. After all four, choose the questions below that interest your group most.
      5. Get ready to share, if you'd like. Sharing with the class is optional.

      All replies here were written for this page to show different levels of sycophancy. They are not real outputs from any model. The tally is just for your group's own discussion.

      Your group's tally, all four situations
      Talk it through
      • Is there a difference between an AI that is warm and an AI that agrees with you? Where would you draw the line?
      • Good feedback is encouraging. When does encouragement turn into sycophancy? What would that look like in an AI tutor talking to your students?
      • In the study, people rated the flattering AI higher. If you were choosing an AI tool for your learners, would you trust their satisfaction ratings to tell you whether it helps them? What would you look at instead?
      • Some users lost something real when GPT-4o was retired. Who should decide how warm an AI is: the company, the user, the teacher, the school?
      If your group would like to share

      Bring one idea from your discussion that you would like the class to hear. There's no need to report your votes.
