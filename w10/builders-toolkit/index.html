Builder’s Toolkit

For turning your design into a prototype: choosing a format, a worked example of building with AI (for pages and games), building an AI assistant, and where to find tools, inspiration and help.

// 01

Choosing a format

The format follows from when, where and on what device your learner will meet it. A phone-friendly page suits a late-night break at work; an assistant suits a learner who gets stuck at odd hours.

Three formats, each with its own breakout room. Mixed formats are welcome; go to the room for the hardest part.

Room 1

Pages

A week’s course page, a lesson, an interactive explanation. Opens on any phone; an AI writes the code with you.

Workflow sample: building a page or a game with AI

One way to work with an AI agent, shown on a small sample project. The same steps work for pages (Room 1) and games (Room 2), with any AI tool you are comfortable with. For a game, see also section 05.

The sample project

Week 1 of a free, four-week online course run by a public library: “Spreadsheets for Your Budget,” for adults who are coming back to learning. The page is the week’s home page.

1

Think it through with AI, then plan

Prompting is a back-and-forth, and the plan takes shape in the conversation. Treat the AI as a colleague to think with, not only a tool that builds. Start broad, say you want to discuss before anything is built, and let the idea, and the format, become clearer as you talk. Your own thinking usually gets clearer too.

prompt · opening · sample
I’m designing part of an online course for a specific learner. Don’t build anything yet; I want to think it through with you first. Here is where I’m starting: [your broad idea, and what you know about the learner].
You lead the conversation
  • Bring what you know about your learner; the AI has never met them.
  • Ask for options, comparisons and objections: “What else could this be?” “What would go wrong for this learner?”
  • This colleague agrees easily. Ask it to argue the other side.
  • The design is yours; the AI suggests and questions.
Close with a plan
  • Ask: “Summarize what we decided as plan.md.”
  • Edit it yourself. Long conversations lose details; the file keeps them for every later step.
  • Check that the plan covers the learner and their context; what it teaches; what the learner does, in order; where people and AI show up; the format and its limits.

What a plan can look like at the end of the conversation:

plan.md · sample
# Plan: Week 1, “Spreadsheets for Your Budget”

Learner: adults in a free online course from a public library. Many have not studied in years. They open the course on a phone in the evening, often for 15–20 minutes at a time.

This week: set up a simple monthly budget sheet (income, expenses, a total with SUM). About one hour across the week.

What the learner does:
1. Watches a short welcome from the librarian who teaches the course.
2. Fills in a five-row budget in a ready-made sheet.
3. Posts one thing that surprised them, and replies to one classmate.

People and AI:
- A weekly roadmap at the top, written by the librarian.
- On Friday the librarian writes a short shout-out naming a few posts and the most common mistake. She uses AI to sort the posts; she writes the note herself.
- An AI formula helper answers questions about formulas and points out errors. It is labeled as AI.
- A short “How AI is used in this course” box at the top.

Format: one web page, phone first, plain language, no login.
2

Explore templates and examples Optional

Look at what already works before you build. Look for two kinds of inspiration: how a page looks and moves (UI), and how good learning content is organized (what comes first, how instructions are written, where people show up).

UI inspiration
  • Land-book and Lapa Ninja: galleries of real web pages.
  • Dribbble: design mockups; search “online course” or “lesson.”
  • H5P examples: interactive activities you can try.
Content inspiration
  • Khan Academy: short explanations with practice right after.
  • OpenStax and OER Commons: open lessons to reuse or adapt.
  • Courses you have taken: which week page did you actually read, and why?

A screenshot of a page you like, pasted into the AI with “use this layout idea,” works better than describing it.

3

Set your preferences

You can give the AI a template, or let it decide the look. Either way, write a short preferences file in your project folder: the AI reads it at the start of every session, so it remembers what you like and what to avoid, even in a new chat.

ToolFile name
Claude Code (any of its apps)CLAUDE.md
CodexAGENTS.md
A chat in the browserPaste it at the start of each new chat, or into a Project’s instructions
CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md · sample
# Preferences for this project

This is a learning design prototype for a graduate course. Read plan.md before every change; it holds the design.

## Always
- One self-contained index.html.
- Phone first: it must work well at 375 px wide.
- Plain language, short paragraphs, clear headings.
- Accessible: headings in order, good color contrast, alt text, works with a keyboard.
- Where a real person speaks (video, message, feedback), leave a visible placeholder like [LIBRARIAN VIDEO: welcome, about 1 minute]. Never write words for a real person.
- Label anything AI does directly for the learner.
- After each change, tell me in two or three lines what you changed.

## Look
- Calm and warm. One accent color. Large buttons.

## Avoid
- No invented names, numbers, quotes or student data.
- No logins, tracking, or forms that send data anywhere.
- Don’t change parts I didn’t ask about.
4

First version, then test

prompt · first version
Read plan.md and CLAUDE.md. Before building, describe the page you will make, section by section, in plain words, so I can check it. When I say go, build index.html and open it in my browser if you can.

(In a browser chat without files: paste your plan and preferences above this prompt, and ask for the complete code in one code block.)

Then open the page as your learner would: make the browser window narrow, or open it on your phone.

Check now
  • It opens and nothing is broken.
  • On a phone, it is readable and easy to tap.
  • The learner can tell what to do first and about how long it takes. In a game: the goal is clear on the first screen, and the feedback teaches something.
  • The main ideas of your plan are visible on the page.
  • Real-person parts are placeholders, and nothing is invented.
Don’t get stuck on
  • Exact colors, fonts and spacing.
  • Perfect wording; content changes in the next rounds anyway.
  • Animation and decoration.
  • Real images; placeholders are fine in a prototype.
  • Small glitches in parts your design doesn’t depend on.
5

Revise, one type of change at a time

Group your changes by type. Mixing a couple in one message is fine at this scale; what matters is keeping each message short. An AI’s memory of a long conversation is weaker than it seems, so when a session gets long or starts forgetting, open a new one: plan.md and your preferences file carry what matters.

UI

How it looks and fits: layout, size, order, phone view.

Function

What it does: buttons, quizzes, feedback, navigation.

Content

What it says: instructions, explanations, the real person’s words.

prompts · samples
UI: On a phone the weekly roadmap takes a whole screen. Make it a short list with the three tasks and their times, and make the buttons bigger.

Function: The formula helper does nothing yet. When the learner clicks it, show the three most common SUM mistakes and how to fix each one. No real AI needed in the prototype; keep the “AI helper” label.

Content: Replace the welcome text with the text below. Keep the librarian video placeholder.
[your text]

Repeat steps 4 and 5 until the page is your final version. When the design changes, ask the AI to update plan.md too.

6

Share, and make it easy to give feedback

Feedback is only as good as what people can find. Before you post, make the page and your post easy to navigate:

  • a clear starting point, and a label on each part saying what it is;
  • what is finished and what is a sketch or placeholder;
  • the one or two questions you want feedback on.

To share a web page by link, see Put it online; screenshots or the file itself also work on the discussion board.

Set up an AI agent Optional

Skip this if you already have a tool you like: any AI you are comfortable with works for this workshop. These are setup notes for anyone who wants an AI agent that writes files on their computer.

Claude Pro or MaxClaude Code is not part of the free plan. Mac and Windows.

  1. Download the Claude app from claude.ai/download, install it, and sign in.

  2. Make an empty folder for the project, for example on your Desktop, named my-lesson.

  3. Click Code at the top of the sidebar. (If the sidebar shows only icons, hover to see the names.)

  4. In the row under the prompt box, open the environment menu and pick the top entry under Local. Then click the folder control next to it, choose Open folder…, and select your project folder.

  5. Type your first prompt and press Enter. When Claude asks to create or edit files, allow it. The permission mode Accept edits stops it from asking for every file.

  6. Open index.html from your folder to see the page in your browser. Ask for changes in the same session, then refresh the browser.

On Windows, if the app says Git is required, update the app first. Newer versions only need Git for advanced features.

Claude Pro or MaxVS Code 1.94 or newer. Mac and Windows.

  1. Install VS Code from code.visualstudio.com.

  2. Open Extensions (Cmd+Shift+X on Mac, Ctrl+Shift+X on Windows), search Claude Code, and install the one published by Anthropic.

  3. Make an empty folder named my-lesson, then in VS Code choose File → Open Folder… and select it.

  4. Open the Claude panel with the Spark icon (✻): in the left Activity Bar, or at the top right of the editor when a file is open. The first time, click Sign in and finish in the browser.

  5. Type your first prompt and press Enter. Claude shows each file change; accept it.

  6. To see the page, right-click index.html in the file list, choose Reveal in Finder (Mac) or Reveal in File Explorer (Windows), and double-click it.

If the extension does not appear, restart VS Code or run Developer: Reload Window from the Command Palette.

Claude Pro or MaxmacOS 13+ or Windows 10+. No other software needed.

  1. Open a terminal: Terminal on Mac (search with Cmd+Space), Windows PowerShell on Windows. Paste the install line for your computer and press Enter.

    Mac · Terminal
    curl -fsSL https://claude.ai/install.sh | bash
    Windows · PowerShell
    irm https://claude.ai/install.ps1 | iex

  2. Close the terminal and open a new one. Check that it worked; you should see a version number.

    Mac and Windows
    claude --version

  3. Make the project folder, go into it, and start Claude. The first time, it opens the browser so you can log in.

    Mac and Windows
    mkdir my-lesson
cd my-lesson
claude

  4. Type your first prompt and press Enter. Claude asks before it creates files; answer yes.

  5. Ask Claude to open the page for you (“Open index.html in my browser”), or type exit and run open index.html (Mac) or start index.html (Windows).

The folder is made in your home folder. On Mac that is your user folder in Finder; on Windows it is C:\Users\your-name.

ChatGPT Free and upFree covers quick tasks; OpenAI does not publish the exact limit. Mac (Apple Silicon) and Windows.

  1. Codex now lives inside the ChatGPT desktop app. Download it from learn.chatgpt.com/docs/app (on Windows it comes from the Microsoft Store) and sign in with your ChatGPT account.

  2. Click Codex in the sidebar.

  3. Make an empty folder named my-lesson and open it as a project (on Windows: Add new project, or Ctrl+O).

  4. Type your first prompt and send it. Approve the file changes when Codex asks.

  5. Open index.html from your folder to see the page. Ask for changes in the same chat, then refresh the browser.

If you reach the Free limit in the middle of the workshop, the “In the browser” tab works with any account.

  1. Open claude.ai, chatgpt.com or gemini.google.com and start a new chat.

  2. Paste your plan and preferences, then your prompt, and ask for “the complete code in one code block.”

  3. Copy the whole code block (the copy button on the block).

// 04

Put it online with GitHub Pages Optional

Right now your page is a file on your computer, so only you can open it. GitHub Pages, a free service, puts it online and gives it a link anyone can open. You can ask your AI agent to do it for you, or do it yourself on the GitHub website. Both ways are below.

A Pages site is public.Anyone with the link can see it. No real student names, grades or personal data, only the learner persona.

With your agent

  1. Create a free GitHub account at github.com/signup.

  2. One time only: ask the agent to set up the tools. You will approve a few installs and log in to GitHub in the browser with a one-time code.

    prompt · setup
    Help me set up Git and the GitHub CLI (gh) on this computer, then log me in to GitHub with gh auth login. Check first what is already installed. Explain each step in plain words and wait for me when I need to do something in the browser.

  3. Ask it to publish:

    prompt · publish
    Publish this folder as a public GitHub Pages site. Create a public repository named my-lesson, push everything on the main branch, turn on GitHub Pages from main at the root, and give me the site link. Ask me before you do anything I need to approve.

  4. Open the link it gives you, which looks like https://your-username.github.io/my-lesson/. The first time can take up to 10 minutes.

  5. After changes, ask: “Commit and push my changes to GitHub.” The site updates within a few minutes.

What the agent runs

For the curious. You never need to type these; this is what you will see the agent ask to run.

terminal
git init
git add .
git commit -m "First version"
git branch -M main
gh repo create my-lesson --public --source . --push
gh api -X POST "repos/{owner}/{repo}/pages" \
  -f "source[branch]=main" -f "source[path]=/"

The last command turns on Pages. gh fills in {owner} and {repo} from the folder’s repository.

Through the GitHub website

  1. On GitHub, click New repository. Name it my-lesson, choose Public, turn on Add README, and click Create repository.

  2. Click Add file → Upload files, drag in index.html and any images, and commit.

  3. Open Settings → Pages. Under Build and deployment, set Source to Deploy from a branch, branch main, folder / (root), and click Save.

  4. Wait up to 10 minutes, then open https://your-username.github.io/my-lesson/.

If the link does not work

You seeCheck
404The file is named exactly index.html (lowercase) and sits at the top of the repository, not in a subfolder. Pages is turned on.
The old versionWait a few minutes, then refresh with Cmd/Ctrl+Shift+R.
Missing imagesThe images were uploaded too, and the file names match exactly, including capital letters.
// 05

Games

A learning game puts the learner in a situation where they act, make choices and see what happens. Build it with the workflow in section 02; this section adds what is specific to games.

Kinds of games that fit an online course

Branching scenario

The learner makes decisions in a realistic situation and sees where each one leads. For example, a new teacher handling a parent email, step by step.

Challenge with levels

Short rounds that get harder, each practicing one skill. For example, find the bug in five lines of Java, then in ten.

Simulation

The learner changes something and watches the result. For example, change the sample size and see the margin of error move.

Puzzle or escape room

The next step unlocks only when the learner applies the idea. For example, match each claim to the study design that could support it.

Decide before you build

  • The learning goal. What the learner can do after playing. The main action in the game should practice exactly that, not decorate it.
  • The goal and rules. Can the learner tell what they are trying to do from the first screen?
  • The challenge. What makes it hard, and how it gets harder.
  • The feedback. After each move, the learner learns why it worked or didn’t, not only “right” or “wrong.”
  • A reason to play again. A new case, a better score, a different path.
  • People. Where other people show up, if anywhere: comparing choices with classmates, a class-wide score, the instructor’s note on the hardest level.
  • Length. A round fits a few minutes on a phone.
game plan · template · add to plan.md
## The game
Kind: [branching scenario / challenge with levels / simulation / puzzle]
Learning goal: after playing, the learner can [ ].
Goal and rules, as the learner reads them on the first screen: [ ]
What makes it hard, and how it gets harder: [ ]
Feedback after each move: [what the learner sees, and what it teaches]
Reason to play again: [ ]
Where people show up: [ ]
One round takes about: [ ] minutes

Build small, then play it

prompt · first round
Read plan.md. Build only the first round of the game as one index.html that works on a phone, so I can play it before you add more. Describe how it will work before you build it.
Check when you play
  • The goal is clear within a few seconds.
  • A wrong move teaches something.
  • You can finish a round on a phone, in a few minutes.
  • Playing it practices the learning goal.
Don’t get stuck on
  • Graphics, sound and animation.
  • Perfectly balanced scores or difficulty.
  • More levels before the first one works.

Without code: Twine makes branching scenarios, and H5P has ready-made quiz and branching formats. More in Resources.

AI assistants

A course assistant the learner talks to, for the whole course or for one week or module of it.

Building a course assistant

An assistant answers the learner directly, without a person checking each reply. The prototype has three parts: its instructions, a few sample conversations, and what the learner is told about it.

assistant instructions · template
You are the course assistant for [course] [or for one week or module of it: what it covers].
The learners: [who they are, when and on what device they use the course].

What you do:
- [for example: explain the course's ideas in plain words, give practice questions, point to the right reading]

What you never do:
- [for example: give answers to graded work, guess at grades or deadlines]

Hand the learner to a real person when: [for example: they mention falling behind, feeling lost, or a personal problem]. Say who that person is and how to reach them: [placeholder].

At the start of every conversation, say in one sentence that you are an AI assistant and what you can help with.
Tone: [for example: warm, brief, one question at a time].
As a GPT
  • In ChatGPT with your USC account or a personal account, open GPTs → Create. If your account doesn’t show Create, use your USC account.
  • Paste your instructions into Configure → Instructions, and test it in the preview next to it.
  • Share the GPT’s link, or screenshots of your test conversations.
As a chatbot built on an API
  • A chat page the learner opens, which sends each message to a model through an API (OpenAI, Anthropic or Google), with your instructions as its system prompt.
  • Ask a coding agent to build it (the setup notes in section 03 work for this too). You need an API key from the provider’s developer platform; use is billed per message.
  • Keep the key private: run the chatbot on your own computer, and never put a key in a page you publish or in a public GitHub repository.

Before building either, a quick test: paste your instructions as the first message of a new chat in any AI, beginning with “For the rest of this chat, act as the assistant described below,” and talk to it as your learner.

Test it as your learner, with at least three conversations: an ordinary question about the course, a moment that should go to a real person, and an attempt to get the answers to graded work. Screenshots or copied text of these conversations are your prototype.

OpenAI is retiring custom GPTs in favor of plugins. Keep your instructions in a file of their own, so the assistant can be rebuilt in another tool.

Resources

A habit that saves trouble later: keep the source link for every image or sound you use, and credit it when the licence asks.

Images and icons

  • Unsplash and Pexels. Free photos.
  • Pixabay. Photos and illustrations.
  • Openverse. Openly licensed images and audio; check each licence.
  • Lucide and Material Symbols. Free icons.

Design and accessibility

  • Google Fonts. Free typefaces.
  • Coolors. Color palettes.
  • WebAIM Contrast Checker. Is the text readable on its background?
  • WAVE. Paste a page link to see accessibility problems.

Interactive pieces and games

  • H5P. Interactive videos, quizzes, drag and drop.
  • Twine. Branching, choose-your-path stories and scenarios.
  • Google Forms. Quick checks and reflections.
  • Padlet. A shared wall; the free plan has 3 padlets.
  • Wayground (formerly Quizizz) and Kahoot. Quizzes and games.

Open content

  • OER Commons and MERLOT. Open lessons and materials.
  • OpenStax. Free textbooks, including Introduction to Sociology and Introductory Statistics.
  • Khan Academy. Videos and practice.

Design guidance

  • CAST UDL Guidelines 3.0. Universal Design for Learning.
  • Quality Matters Higher Education Rubric. Standards for online course design.
  • W3C WAI design tips. Short accessibility guidance.

Other places to host

  • Netlify Drop. Drag a folder in for an instant link; claim it with an account to keep it.
  • Google Sites. No code at all; school accounts may limit public sharing.

Help

Questions about your format: go to that format’s breakout room. For an error message, paste it to your AI and ask: “Explain this problem in plain words, then fix it.”
